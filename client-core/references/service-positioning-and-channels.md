# Service Positioning and Acquisition Channels

## Minimum viable positioning

Define a service narrowly enough that a buyer can recognize relevance:

- target situation and buyer;
- costly or frustrating problem;
- observable deliverable or outcome;
- suitable project size and constraints;
- truthful proof available now;
- explicit exclusions and referral boundary;
- lowest-friction next step.

Prefer “build and launch a Telegram booking workflow for a small service business” over “full-stack developer using React and .NET.” Technology supports credibility but is not the buyer's outcome.

Do not delay acquisition to build a large personal brand or perfect portfolio. A concise profile, one or two honest demonstrations, a clear offer, and a reliable conversation process are enough to test a channel.

## Proof ladder

Use the strongest truthful evidence available:

1. paid client outcome with permission;
2. shipped real project;
3. relevant open-source contribution;
4. working demonstration using realistic constraints;
5. technical teardown or prototype;
6. process evidence: specification, tests, deployment, documentation;
7. relevant prior work with limitations disclosed;
8. explicit “new to this exact niche” statement plus a risk-controlled first step.

Never transform a tutorial, generated mockup, school task, unpaid concept, team result, or confidential work into a paid personal case study.

## Select channels experimentally

Consider:

- warm network and referrals;
- partnerships with designers, marketers, consultants, and small agencies;
- relevant local or professional communities;
- carefully selected marketplaces;
- targeted responses to public requests;
- useful public content or open-source work;
- narrow, researched outbound where lawful and welcome.

For each channel record:

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Audience fit | Does the channel contain the target buyer and problem? |
| Trust path | What evidence is expected before a conversation? |
| Time cost | Research, profile work, bidding, and follow-up effort |
| Cash cost | Fees, commissions, subscriptions, and payment friction |
| Speed | Typical time to a meaningful reply or call |
| Control | Dependence on rankings, platform rules, or intermediaries |
| Relationship value | Chance of referrals or repeat work |
| Risk | Spam, fraud, nonpayment, policy, geography, or data concerns |

Rules, pricing, access, and popularity change. Verify current channel claims against the platform's official documentation before recommending meaningful time or money.

## Opportunity triage

Prefer an opportunity when:

- the problem and decision path are plausible;
- the buyer can participate and supply dependencies;
- the work matches current capability or can begin with a bounded discovery;
- acceptance can be made observable;
- commercial and geographic constraints are workable;
- the opportunity cost is justified.

Decline or investigate further when the request involves illegality, deception, abusive surveillance, counterfeit engagement, credential sharing, hidden subcontracting, impossible guarantees, pressure to bypass security, unpaid speculative delivery, or a refusal to record basic terms.

## Outreach standard

Every first contact should contain:

1. a factual reason this specific person or company is relevant;
2. a modest hypothesis, not a declaration of their pain;
3. one credible connection to the service;
4. one low-friction question or next step;
5. a respectful exit.

Keep it short. Do not use fake familiarity, scraped private data, automated personalization theater, tracking tricks, manipulative follow-up, or mass sequences disguised as personal messages.

Measure meaningful outcomes by channel: qualified replies, useful calls, proposals requested, wins, time spent, effective acquisition cost, project quality, and repeat/referral value. Raw message volume is not success.
